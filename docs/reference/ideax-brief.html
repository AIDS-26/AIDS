The story

Picture a mall's CCTV room. Sensors raise an alarm every few seconds, and most alarms are a door swinging open. After a week the guards stop looking. Then a real break-in rings an alarm, and nobody checks it.

Company security teams live in that room. Industry surveys put the average at about 3,000 security alerts a day, with most of them never read. The sensors already see the attacks. The problem is that people cannot read everything the sensors raise.

IDEA-X does not build a better sensor. It changes how the alarms reach people:

  • Group repeated alarms about the same target into one alert, so a flood of 756 identical warnings becomes one line.
  • Escalate that one alert when the repeats keep coming, so a target being hit again and again rises to the top.
  • Let Codex tune the settings for each kind of alarm, the way a site reliability engineer tunes on-call alarms: measure the flow, compare it with what the team can read, and adjust.
  • Check every change with a fixed safety check before it is used, and keep a record of what changed and why.

Words you will hear

From security

SOC
Security Operations Center. The team, or room, that watches alerts and decides which ones matter. The CCTV room in the story.
Suricata
Free, open-source software that watches network traffic and raises an alert when traffic matches one of its rules. It is our sensor. We do not change its rules.
PCAP
A recording of network traffic. The CCTV footage.
eve.json
The file Suricata writes. One line per alert: time, which rule fired, source IP, target IP, port. The PDF calls our sample eve_demo_FINAL.json (5,483 alerts).
CIC-IDS2017
A public research dataset: a week of recorded traffic from a test company network, with real attacks run at known times. We use the Thursday recording. Because the attack times are published, we know which alerts belong to real attacks. Those are the labels.
Attack vs benign
Attack: the alert came from one of the known attacks. Benign: harmless traffic that still tripped a rule, such as a policy warning.
SIEM
The dashboard that collects every alert and decides what to show or email. The PDF has two: SIEM I (original rules) and SIEM II (tuned rules). In the product we call them Original rules and Tuned rules.
Alert fatigue
When people stop reading alerts because there are too many.
Severity band
Scores run from 1 to 10. Low 1–4, Medium 5–7, High 8–10. Each High alert in the original rules sends an email.

From SRE (site reliability engineering)

SRE
The engineers who keep an app like Shopee running. Their habit: watch the traffic, set a limit for what people can handle, and tune the alarms so on-call staff are not woken up by noise.
Budget B
How many alerts per hour the analysts can actually read. An operator sets this number, for example 20 per hour.
Burn rate
Alerts arriving per hour divided by the budget. 373 per hour against a budget of 20 gives a burn rate of 18.6×: the team gets almost 19 times what it can read.
p95
The 95th percentile. “p95 burst size is 40” means 95% of bursts have 40 events or fewer. It describes a normal busy moment while ignoring the rare extreme ones.
Delta gap
Observed alerts per hour minus the share of the budget a rule deserves. Positive: the rule is too noisy. Negative: an important rule is too quiet.

Our system

Rule
In the PDF a “rule” is an attack type at a service, for example “Brute force at web server”. Each gets its own settings.
Window W
How many minutes the tuned rules wait to collect repeats into the same group. Bigger W means more grouping and fewer alerts.
N_sat
The saturation point: how many events in a group push its score all the way to 10. Smaller N_sat means quicker escalation.
Group key
What makes two alerts “the same”: same target and same attack type. Scanners are the exception: they are grouped by who is scanning (the source), because they hit many targets.
Codex tuner
Codex, run offline before the tuned replay. It reads the original results and writes new W and N_sat values per rule into tuned_rules.yaml, with a reason for each change. It never scores alerts itself.
evaluate()
A fixed script Codex calls to test its own proposal: how many fewer alerts, did every attack still show up, was any harmless group raised.
Guardrail
A fixed safety check that runs after Codex. It rejects a proposal that breaks a limit, for example a grouping window that is too long for a dangerous rule.
Recall
Of the real attacks in the labels, how many still appear correctly after tuning. Must be 100%. Exact definition to confirm with the scorecard owner.
YAML
A plain-text settings format that is easy to read and to compare line by line (a “diff”).

How one alert gets its score

S = min(10, A + C)
  • A is the attack-type weight: how dangerous this kind of attack is. An exploit weighs more than a ping.
  • C is the service weight: how important the target machine is, taken from the asset map. The web server matters more than a test laptop.
  • 10 is the cap. No score goes above it.

The original rules stop here: every alert gets its S and is shown by itself. If S is 8 or more, it sends an email. That is how 756 exploit alerts became 756 emails in the PDF.

How grouping raises the score

The tuned rules collect alerts with the same group key inside the window W. The group's score rises with the number of events in it, n:

X = round( S + (10 − S) × min(1, ln n ÷ ln N_sat) )

In words: start at the base score S and close the distance to 10. The more events, the more of the distance you close. At N_sat events you reach 10. The logarithm (ln) makes the first few repeats count most: going from 1 to 10 events matters more than going from 100 to 110.

Two safety facts come from the shape of the formula. With one event, ln 1 = 0, so X = S: nothing is ever scored lower than its base. And benign alerts are excluded on purpose: they always keep S.

Bars: the score this S and N_sat would give at n = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 60, 200 events.

One example in the PDF needs care. The PDF's one-page primer says “two minor alarms on the same server become one medium alarm.” That is only true when N_sat is small. With S = 3 and N_sat = 60, two events give 4, still Low. It takes N_sat of 25 or less. Try it above with S = 3 and n = 2. Say “repeats raise the score” in the pitch instead, or show a case that is true.

What Codex calculates

Codex treats the alert stream like website traffic. It does five things for each rule, then proposes settings.

  1. Measure the flow. Alerts per hour from the original run, plus the biggest and typical (p95) burst inside a window.
  2. Compare with the budget. All rules together send 373 alerts per hour. The budget is 20. Burn rate 18.6×.
  3. Give each rule a weight. w = S × evidence. Evidence is 1.0 for attack traffic and 0.2 for benign traffic, so harmless noise gets a small share.
  4. Split the budget by weight. Allowed per hour = B × w ÷ (sum of all w).
  5. Find the gap. Observed per hour − allowed per hour.

You can change the budget below and watch the shares move. The observed numbers are from the PDF. The base scores are back-calculated from the PDF's allowed column, so treat them as a reconstruction.

What Codex changes

  • Positive gap (too noisy): pick the smallest window W that brings the rule's grouped alerts under its allowed share. Set N_sat to the rule's p95 burst size, so a normal busy burst reaches 10 and anything bigger stands out.
  • Negative gap (too quiet for its importance): keep W small so the alert fires at once, and make it escalate sooner. In the sample, “Exploit at Windows Vista host” is the one rule like this.
  • Hard limit: rules with S of 8 or more never get a long window, so a serious attack is never delayed by grouping.

How Codex checks itself

After writing tuned_rules.yaml, Codex runs evaluate() and reads three numbers: alert reduction, attack recall and benign groups raised. If recall is below 100% or any benign group was raised, it revises and runs again, at most three times. Then the guardrail checks the final proposal. Codex is only allowed to edit that one YAML file.

In the product, Codex runs through the Codex CLI (codex exec), started by our app. The screen shows it as a job with a log, the way a build server shows a build.

Limits we say out loud

  • Labels at runtime. The tuned rules use the dataset's attack/benign labels to keep benign alerts at their base score and to set the 0.2 evidence weight. A real system would not have these labels. We present them as a stand-in for analyst feedback, and the screen says so.
  • The budget and the 0.2 weight are assumptions. They are operator inputs, not facts.
  • One dataset, one day. A clean result on Thursday's traffic is not a promise about future attacks.
  • No response actions. IDEA-X reads alerts and sends emails. It does not block IPs or touch other systems.
  • Privacy. Codex is a cloud model. We send it counts per rule, not raw traffic.

Keeping it from looking AI-made

  • No purple, gradients, glows, sparkle or robot icons, chat bubbles or typing effects. The brand blue does the work; red only means High.
  • Codex is shown like a build job: a status, a log, a result. No “AI assistant” framing.
  • Flat white surfaces, 1-pixel borders, small corner radius, no drop shadows.
  • Dense tables and real units: IPs, timestamps, events per hour. Few big hero numbers.
  • Color is for meaning only: red High, amber Medium, green Low, grey benign.

QA checklist for the tuned output

Your QA covers the tuned rules and the grouped alerts. These are checks that catch the usual mistakes.

  • Nothing lost. Every original alert belongs to exactly one group. The sum of group event counts equals the original count.
  • Never below base. No group scores lower than the base S of its events.
  • Benign stays put. Every benign group keeps its base S. “Benign groups raised” is 0.
  • Group keys. Scanners (port scan, ping sweep) are grouped by source. Everything else by target and attack type.
  • N_sat is 2 or more. With N_sat = 1, ln N_sat is 0 and the formula divides by zero.
  • Same rounding everywhere. Python's round(4.5) gives 4. JavaScript's Math.round(4.5) gives 5. If the backend scores in Python and the screen recomputes in JavaScript, a band can flip. Pick one rule and have only one side compute.
  • What the window means. Agree whether a group stays open while events keep arriving within W minutes of the last one, or closes W minutes after the first. The PDF's replay kept 756 exploit events in one group, which only happens with the first meaning. Also agree what happens to an event exactly W minutes later.
  • Long windows on serious rules. Rules with S of 8 or more stay within the maximum window.
  • Numbers match. The figures on screen equal the evaluate output for the same run.
